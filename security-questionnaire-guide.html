Guide · Customer Security Questionnaires

Your customer isn’t collecting forms. They’re collecting proof.

What It Is

A procurement gate, not a paperwork exercise.

A security questionnaire is how a customer decides whether you are a safe pair of hands for their data. It arrives dressed as a spreadsheet, or a login to a portal, but the decision behind it is commercial: can this business be trusted inside our supply chain, with our customers’ information in its systems? Treat it as paperwork and it stalls your deal at the last step. Treat it as what it is — a request for evidence — and it can move the deal forward.

The sender is rarely trying to catch you out. Their own customers, insurers, and funders ask them the same questions about their vendors, and your answers become part of their file — the record they would produce if anyone asked why they chose you. A weak response doesn’t just raise a question about your security. It leaves the person championing your deal with nothing to defend you with.

When It Lands

Triage it before you answer a single question.

The first hour with a questionnaire decides whether the rest is orderly or a scramble. Five moves, before anyone types an answer:

Move one
Give it one owner. Not a committee, and not simply “the IT person.” One named person who tracks every question, every attachment, and the deadline — and who has the standing to ask colleagues for documents and actually get them.
Move two
Find the real deadline. The date in the covering email, minus the sender’s review time, minus your own approval step. If the date is impossible, say so on day one and agree a new one. Senders respect an early conversation; they remember a silent miss.
Move three
Count and sort before you answer. Group the questions: company and policy questions, technical questions, and requests for attachments. Under the branding, most questionnaires are the same eight areas in different wording — the sections below. Sorting them turns a wall of questions into a short evidence list.
Move four
Spot the evidence hiding inside the yes/no questions. “Do you test your restores?” is not asking for a tick; it is asking for a date and a record. Read every yes as the start of a follow-up — show me. If the proof would take more than a day to produce, that question is work, not admin.
Move five
Ask your questions early. An ambiguous question, evidence you don’t hold in the format requested, a section that doesn’t apply to your business — a short clarification email on day one is professional. The same email on the deadline day is an excuse.
The Evidence Principle

Every “yes” is a claim. Claims need documents.

The test for every answer is simple: could you produce the proof within one business day? If the document exists — a dated policy, a coverage report, a test record — the yes is safe, and the evidence can travel with it. If producing the proof would take a week of reconstruction, the honest answer is not yes. It is the truth, with a date and an owner.

The bare yes
“Yes — MFA is enabled.” Nothing behind it: no scope, no date, no record. It answers the question on the form and invites exactly the follow-up it cannot support.
The answer that holds up
“MFA is enforced on email and remote access today — coverage report attached. Administrator accounts complete on [date], owned by [name].” Same control, honestly scoped: what is done, what is in progress, when it finishes, and who owns it. Reviewers trust this answer precisely because it admits its edges.

That is not underselling yourself. A dated, owned “in progress” tells the reviewer your business manages its security deliberately. A bare “yes” that collapses under one follow-up tells them the opposite — and questionnaires have a way of coming back out of the file: at renewal, at audit, after an incident.

The Eight Areas

What the sections ask — and the proof that counts.

Strip the branding off any customer’s questionnaire and the same eight areas appear, in some order, in some wording. For each: the question behind the questions, and the evidence that answers it. If you can produce the proof within one business day, you are ready for that section. If you can only describe it, that section is your work plan.

1Know what you have

They ask what systems you run, what software you rely on, and what customer or client data you hold. The proof that counts: a current inventory of computers, servers, devices, cloud services and software — with who administers each — and a data map showing where that data lives. You cannot defend what you have not listed.

2Control who gets in

They ask who can reach your systems, and how access is granted and taken away. The proof that counts: MFA enforced on email, remote access, and administrator accounts; a named account for every user, with no shared logins; a current list of administrator rights; a written joiner/leaver process; and a dated record of your most recent access review.

3Keep systems defended

They ask whether your systems are patched and protected, and whether you go looking for weaknesses. The proof that counts: a patching schedule with the most recent patch report, endpoint protection on every device with a coverage list to prove it, a recent vulnerability scan with its results, and email filtering and anti-phishing protection in place.

4Back up — and prove recovery

They ask how you back up — and the only part that really matters: whether you have ever restored. The proof that counts: a documented backup schedule (what, how often, where), backups protected against ransomware, a retention period that matches your policy, and a dated record of a restore test. A schedule is a claim; a restore test is evidence.

5Be ready for an incident

They ask what happens on your worst day. The proof that counts: a written incident response plan, however short; the contact list for the first phone calls — IT provider, insurer, bank, legal counsel; notes from a tabletop exercise, a real incident, or lessons learned; and your cyber insurance policy details page, if you carry cover.

6Govern it on paper

They ask whether security in your business is managed or improvised. The proof that counts: an information security policy that is dated and signed, your most recent risk assessment, a risk register naming each significant risk, its owner, and its treatment — and a remediation tracker showing findings closed, not just opened.

7Prepare your people

They ask whether your staff are part of the defence or part of the gap. The proof that counts: security awareness training records, phishing-awareness activity or results if you run them, signed acceptable-use acknowledgments, and background checks where a role or a contract requires them.

8Manage your vendors

They ask how you vet the businesses that can reach your systems or your data — the same judgement they are making about you. The proof that counts: an inventory of those vendors, signed data-protection or business-associate agreements where they apply, a security review or questionnaire response on file for critical vendors, and breach-notification commitments in the contracts.

Red Flags

Five ways firms talk themselves into trouble.

Mistake one
Answering aspirationally. “We are implementing MFA” written as “yes” is a misrepresentation, and it tends to surface — at renewal, at audit, or after an incident, when the questionnaire comes back out of the file. Answer for the day you sign, not the quarter you hope for.
Mistake two
Naming tools you don’t run. Listing a product because it was quoted, trialled, or installed on one laptop invites the follow-up — show me the coverage report — and that answer ends the conversation about your credibility, not just about that question.
Mistake three
Promising dates you can’t keep. A remediation date on a questionnaire is a commitment the sender may well diarise. Before you write one down, put an owner and a realistic schedule behind it. A missed promised date reads worse than a later, honest one.
Mistake four
Sending evidence you don’t have. A screenshot of a settings page that isn’t yours, a policy template with the blanks still in it, a report from the wrong period. Thin evidence is worse than none at all, because it tells the reviewer to check everything else twice.
Mistake five
Missing the deadline silently. Deals rarely die on one weak answer; they die on drift. If you need more time, ask early, say what is being assembled, and offer the sections you have finished. Partial and punctual beats complete and late.
The Workflow

A response that fits in days, not weeks.

For a typical questionnaire, this rhythm works — and most of the time goes on evidence you already have, once someone goes and gets it.

1Day one — triage and assign

Name the owner, confirm the deadline, sort the questions into the eight areas, and list the evidence each section will need. Send your clarification questions to the sender today — not at the end.

2Days one to two — assemble what exists

Collect the documents that already exist: policies, reports, records, agreements. Note the gaps plainly as you find them. A gap found on day two is a work item; the same gap found on deadline day is a crisis.

3Days two to three — draft, honestly

Write the answers with the evidence attached. Where the truth is “in progress,” write it with a date and an owner. Keep the wording factual and short — reviewers read a great many of these, and clarity is itself a credibility signal.

4Day four — check, send, and file

Have a second person read the answers against the evidence, tracing every yes back to its document. Then send — and file the exact response and attachments you sent. The next questionnaire starts from that file, not from memory.
